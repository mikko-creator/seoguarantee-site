## §2 persona-pillars — M
Exactly 3 pillars, one short paragraph each, written to an owner who quotes work and chases bids. Competitor displacement is the frame.

Pillar 1 — the job is won at the estimate request, not at the click. This trade's conversion event is a form, a photo, a scope and a callback, not an impulse phone call. Traffic that never becomes a request is not a partial success in contracting, it is nothing, and a report full of sessions is exactly the kind of number this page refuses to sell on.

Pillar 2 — trade-and-metro phrases are named in the contract before you sign. One line here; the path section below carries the detail. The agency proposes the set from competitive research and the contractor signs it. There is no veto and no substitution right.

Pillar 3 — a long consideration cycle is precisely why the assessment is monthly. This is the pillar that inverts the usual objection. In a trade where a homeowner takes six weeks between first search and signed contract, "give it time" is the easiest thing in the world for an agency to say and the hardest thing for a client to argue with. A monthly assessment against named phrases removes that argument in both directions: the position is either held this month or it is not, whatever the revenue lag happens to be. Off-target, the billing stops and the work continues.

## §3 capability-deep-dive — the search-to-estimate path — M
The section that makes this a real contracting page, and the only vertical brief on this site that leads with conversion rather than with phrases alone. Build it as a path with two halves that are contracted together.

Half one, the phrase set. Structure the demand the way it actually splits:
(a) Trade-and-metro phrases — the trade name plus a city or a suburb. The core commercial set, heavily contested, and where the metro's established firms already sit.
(b) Job-scoped phrases — a specific scope of work plus a location. Lower volume per phrase, far higher intent, and the tier where a contractor's actual specialism separates them from a general listing.
(c) Cost and research phrases — what a job costs, how long it takes, what is involved. Very large volume, poor direct conversion, and valuable because they reach a buyer weeks before they are ready to request anything. Say plainly that these are a different job from the commercial set and should be contracted as such, not blended into one promise.
(d) Homeowner versus commercial and general-contractor demand — two audiences that share a trade name and share almost no phrases. A firm serving both is running two sets.

Half two, the estimate request itself, which is included in the same agreed fee and is where this vertical differs from every other page on this site. Conversion work here is concrete and checkable: the request form and how much it asks for before it lets someone submit, whether photos can be attached, whether a scope can be described in the buyer's own words, how fast the callback is promised and whether that promise is kept, and the project gallery a homeowner uses to decide whether you build things that look like the thing they want. Describe this as scope of work. Do not attach a conversion-rate figure to it — no such figure exists anywhere in the corpus.

Tie the two halves to the owner's own stated objectives for the build, in his sequence: get traffic, generate high quality leads, convert those leads to sales. This page is the one place in the vertical set where the middle term carries the weight, because in contracting the gap between a visit and a lead is where the money is lost.

Method of proposal: competitive research against the firms currently holding page one for each candidate phrase, then a proposed list, then a signed list. Signing is what makes "delivered" checkable instead of arguable.

Any example phrase must read as illustrative of the SHAPE. No verified contracting phrase string exists in the captured corpus, so nothing here may be presented as a position the agency holds or has held.

OWNER-PENDING (§G-2 residual, and §G-4, both acute on this page): how many phrases the contract names, how the service area is defined, who verifies the monthly measurement — and, more sharply here than anywhere else, exactly where the conversion work stops. The estimate-request path crosses into build and integration work that may or may not be in scope, and what is NOT included has never been stated anywhere.
